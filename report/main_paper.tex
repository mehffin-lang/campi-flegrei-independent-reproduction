Campi Flegrei's unrest is recorded by ground displacement and seismicity. Tan et al. (2025) use a machine-learning-assisted earthquake catalog to resolve active structures and evaluate possible sources of unrest. Their study is observational: its central results describe the location, timing and character of earthquakes, rather than solutions of a coupled physical forward model. Here, the completed independent calculations are placed in the same scientific sequence. Published images are confined to the separate comparison PDF.

\section*{The current phase of unrest}
Figure 1 reconstructs the historical context from the explicitly permitted routine catalog and original displacement observations. The routine snapshot contains 16,046 located events from 2005 through 20 March 2025, including five events with duration magnitude at least 4.0. Monthly counts include all routine events; the cumulative series includes 7,218 events with $M_d>0.2$. These external records are separate from the independently detected catalog.

The displacement reconstruction contains 128 leveling entries and 8,743 daily GNSS observations through 20 March 2025. The original 1905--2023 interval is extended with observed data, and the extension is shaded. A common vertical datum is obtained from overlapping observation records. The long sequence resolves subsidence, the major twentieth-century uplift episodes, and the recent uplift. It does not by itself distinguish magmatic from hydrothermal forcing. All three panels remain partial because the historical catalog snapshot, basemap and exact displacement-stitching convention differ or are incompletely specified.

\section*{Improving the earthquake catalog}
Independent PhaseNet inference and GaMMA association yield \CurrentEvents{} provisional events in the processed waveform sample. These are initial associations with an alternative starting velocity profile. A limited VELEST pilot is reported in the supplement, but regional calibration and double-difference relocation have not established a catalog comparable to Tan et al.'s result. Consequently, they cannot support an independent multiyear seismicity rate or depth--time distribution (Figure 2).

\section*{A clearer view of the current phase of unrest}
Figures 3 and 4 address the publication's main structural observations: the distribution of seismicity and the source mechanisms of larger events. The present independent work has no calibrated, relocated catalog or focal-mechanism solutions. It therefore cannot determine the depth range, continuity or temporal activation of the ring fault, resolve the central fault system, or establish its slip orientations. The placeholders retain these scientific questions without inserting provisional diagnostic locations or supplied author solutions.

\section*{Implications for the physical process driving the unrest}
The reconstructed hybrid-template example in Figure S15C is an independently processed waveform observation. The limited template search produced \CurrentHybridCandidates{} unconfirmed threshold crossings; it does not establish recurring hybrid families or their evolution. Neither this result nor the initial associations can test the publication's inference about the presence or absence of upward seismic migration. The partial displacement history also does not distinguish magmatic and hydrothermal forcing. Such an interpretation would require the unresolved spatial and source constraints above.

\section*{New perspectives for improved seismic risk assessment}
Fault geometry and a calibrated magnitude distribution are needed to connect this analysis to seismic risk. The pilot magnitude measurements have not established the multiyear magnitude-frequency distribution required for Figure S8. The current results therefore provide no revised estimate of event probabilities, damaging-event locations or source dimensions. Their supported contribution is restricted to the partial observational reconstructions shown here and in the supplement.
